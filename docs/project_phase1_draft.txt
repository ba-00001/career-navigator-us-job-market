ISM 4402 Group Project
Phase 1 Draft

Project Topic
U.S. Entry-Level Job Market by Sector: Building a Career Navigator Framework

Project Framing
This project analyzes the U.S. entry-level job market across sectors to identify where early-career workers are most likely to find opportunities, what occupations are growing fastest, what wages are available, what education is typically required, and how sector characteristics differ across the labor market. The final business framing connects these findings to the idea of a LinkedIn Career Navigator AI that helps users choose sectors, occupations, and learning paths based on labor-market evidence.

Section 1 - Description of Data

Summary of the data chosen
The project will use a multi-table dataset assembled from real U.S. labor-market sources, primarily the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics and O*NET. This is a better fit than using a single small dataset because the course emphasizes connecting to data, transforming data, joining tables, exploratory analysis, clustering, forecasting, dashboards, and storytelling.

The proposed data stack includes:

1. BLS Employment Projections data tables
- Occupational projections and worker characteristics
- Industry-occupation matrix data by occupation
- Industry-occupation matrix data by industry
- Occupational separations and openings
- Employment and output by industry

2. BLS Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics tables
- National industry-specific and by ownership
- All data

3. BLS State and Area Employment annual average tables
- Employees on nonfarm payrolls in states and selected areas by major industry

4. BLS CPS labor force characteristics tables
- Average hours at work by industry
- Average hours at work by occupation

5. O*NET occupation attributes
- Work Activities.xlsx

Why this data is appropriate
This combined dataset is large, complex, real, and highly relevant to the course. It supports:
- descriptive analytics for data exploration
- predictive analytics using growth and time-based sector trends
- calculations and derived fields
- transforming data through joins, pivots, filtering, and standardization
- dashboards and stories in Tableau
- clustering based on job characteristics
- forecasting using sector employment trends

What needs to be done to prepare the data

The data will require substantial preparation before analysis:

1. Standardize occupation codes
Occupation data from BLS and O*NET use SOC or O*NET-SOC codes. These fields must be cleaned and standardized so the occupation-level tables can be joined correctly.

2. Standardize sector and industry labels
BLS industry tables use industry codes and sector names. These must be cleaned into a consistent sector dimension so that wages, growth, employment, and openings can be compared across sectors.

3. Filter for entry-level occupations
The project focus is entry-level labor-market opportunities. Occupations will be filtered using worker-characteristic fields such as typical entry-level education, work experience in a related occupation, and typical on-the-job training. Occupations with lower barriers to entry will be flagged for the core analysis.

4. Create calculated fields
The analysis will likely require calculated fields such as:
- entry-level flag
- wage percentile group
- projected growth category
- openings per occupation
- sector growth ranking
- occupation skill intensity score
- sector attractiveness score

5. Handle missing values and suppressed values
Some tables may contain blanks, suppressed values, or non-comparable categories. These will be documented and handled through filtering, null handling, or exclusion where appropriate.

6. Join multiple tables
The project will join occupation-level, industry-level, and sector/state-level tables. Likely join keys include occupation code, industry code, and state or geography fields depending on the worksheet.

7. Reshape some fields for Tableau
O*NET Work Activities data is likely in long format with repeated rows by occupation and activity. Some activity subsets may need to be pivoted or aggregated depending on the visual.

8. Build analysis-ready subsets
Instead of using every raw column in one wide table, the project should create focused analysis tables for:
- occupations
- sectors
- state-sector employment
- occupation attributes
- occupation-sector mapping

Proposed tables and unit of analysis

Table 1: Occupation Projections
Lowest level of granularity: one row per occupation
Purpose: growth, wages, openings, education, training, work experience

Table 2: Industry Employment and Output
Lowest level of granularity: one row per industry
Purpose: sector size, projected growth, output change

Table 3: Industry-Occupation Matrix
Lowest level of granularity: one row per occupation within industry, or one row per industry within occupation depending on selected version
Purpose: which occupations are concentrated in which sectors

Table 4: OEWS Wages by Occupation and Industry
Lowest level of granularity: one row per occupation-industry combination
Purpose: wage comparison across sectors and occupations

Table 5: State Employment by Major Industry
Lowest level of granularity: one row per state-sector combination
Purpose: geographic distribution of opportunities

Table 6: O*NET Work Activities
Lowest level of granularity: one row per occupation-activity-scale combination
Purpose: clustering and skill-profile analysis

Section 2 - Purpose of the Analysis

Overall purpose of the analysis
The purpose of the analysis is to understand where the strongest entry-level opportunities exist in the U.S. job market and how those opportunities vary by sector, occupation, wages, projected growth, education requirements, and work characteristics. The group will use this analysis to support the idea of a LinkedIn Career Navigator AI that helps early-career users choose a sector and occupation path based on real market data.

Angle of the analysis
The most interesting angle is not just "which jobs pay the most." Instead, the project will evaluate opportunity from multiple perspectives:
- accessibility for entry-level workers
- projected growth and openings
- sector demand
- wage potential
- work activity patterns
- geography

This creates a stronger Tableau story because it allows the group to compare sectors and occupations using both business and career-planning criteria.

Guiding questions for the analysis

1. Which U.S. sectors currently offer the strongest volume of employment for entry-level workers?
2. Which entry-level occupations have the highest projected growth and annual openings?
3. How do wages for similar entry-level occupations differ across sectors?
4. Which sectors combine stronger wage potential with stronger projected employment growth?
5. How do education and training requirements vary across entry-level occupations and sectors?
6. Which states show the strongest concentration of employment in sectors that are attractive for early-career workers?
7. Can occupations be clustered into meaningful groups based on work activities and job characteristics?
8. Which occupations appear most attractive when balancing accessibility, wages, and growth?

Preliminary recommended scope for Tableau
To keep the project manageable while still showing complexity, the final Tableau story should focus on:
- major U.S. sectors
- entry-level occupations only
- selected high-interest sectors such as technology-related services, finance, healthcare, professional services, retail, and logistics
- occupation clusters and sector comparisons

Recommended final narrative
The final narrative can be:
"Where should an early-career worker start? A data-driven look at entry-level opportunities across U.S. sectors."
